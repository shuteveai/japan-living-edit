A pour-over dripper usually lets water leave as soon as gravity and the coffee bed allow it.

That makes flow part of the brewing process from the first pour. Water enters at the top, moves through ground coffee, passes through the filter, and reaches the cup or server below.

HARIO's [Immersion Dripper SWITCH SSD-200-B](https://shop.hariocorp.co.jp/products/ssd-200-b) changes that sequence by adding a small control mechanism. The brewer still uses familiar V60 geometry, but the switch changes when water is allowed to leave the dripper.

That is the useful design question:

**Why does adding a switch change the brewing process?**

This is not a hands-on review, a recipe recommendation, or a claim that the SWITCH makes better coffee than a conventional V60. It is a fact-based editorial guide to how a small physical control changes the workflow of a brewing tool.

## 1. Why Flow Timing Matters

In a conventional dripper, water begins moving through the coffee bed as it is poured.

That movement is part of the method. The brewer, paper filter, grind, pouring pattern, and coffee bed all interact while water continues downward.

The SWITCH introduces a different sequence.

HARIO describes the product as an immersion dripper: coffee grounds are immersed in hot water, and then the switch is pressed to start dripping. The official product page describes the basic operation as adding coffee grounds to the paper filter, pouring hot water, allowing immersion, and pressing the switch after approximately two minutes so the coffee begins to drain.

The important point is not the exact time as a universal recipe.

It is that the brewer creates a pause between pouring and draining.

That pause changes the task from continuous flow toward a hold-and-release workflow.

## 2. What Changes When a Dripper Can Stop the Flow

A small control can change the role of the brewer.

Without a stop mechanism, the dripper is mostly a passage. Water enters, meets coffee, and continues through the filter.

With the SWITCH, the dripper can temporarily hold water and coffee together. The switch is not decorative; it changes the timing relationship between pouring, immersion, and draining.

That is the central JLE interpretation:

**A small control mechanism changes when water is allowed to leave the brewer.**

The product does not need to be framed as a replacement for every other brewing method. It is more useful to understand the design difference.

One approach emphasizes continuous movement.

The other adds a deliberate stop-and-release step.

## 3. The Switch-and-Ball Mechanism

The SSD-200-B is built around a simple physical idea: stop the opening, then release it.

HARIO lists the switch material as PCT resin and the ball as stainless steel. The official description presents the switch as the action that starts dripping after the coffee has been immersed.

Japan Living Edit should not expand that into an internal engineering claim.

The useful level of explanation is workflow-based. The brewer gives the user a physical step between adding water and allowing the liquid to drain. The switch-and-ball mechanism is the part of the object that makes that sequence possible.

This matters because it makes the control visible.

The user is not only pouring water into a cone. The user is also deciding when the brewer changes from holding to draining.

That is the design change.

## 4. Hold, Immerse, Release

The SWITCH workflow can be summarized as:

**Pour → Hold → Immerse → Switch → Release**

HARIO's official product page describes putting coffee grounds in the paper filter, pouring hot water, waiting through an immersion period, and then pressing the switch to drip.

The page refers to pressing the switch after approximately two minutes. That timing should be treated as HARIO's stated operation guidance, not as Japan Living Edit's universal brewing recipe.

Coffee brewing still depends on many variables: grind size, dose, water amount, water temperature, filter, coffee, and the cup or server below.

This article is not evaluating flavor.

The design point is narrower. The brewer separates the time when water is poured from the time when coffee begins draining. That turns flow timing into an explicit action rather than something that happens immediately throughout the pour.

## 5. Continuous Flow vs Controlled Release

The contrast with a conventional dripper is easiest to understand through the path of water.

In a continuous-flow setup, water keeps moving once it enters the brewer. The user's actions during pouring influence how water moves through the bed.

In the SWITCH, HARIO's described method includes a holding period before the switch is pressed and coffee begins to drain.

That difference does not prove one method is inherently preferable.

It simply changes what the brewer asks the user to manage.

A conventional V60-style workflow places more attention on active pouring while flow is already underway. The SWITCH adds a physical pause, so the timing of release becomes part of the process.

For readers thinking about a small home coffee setup, the relevant question is not whether one brewer is more advanced.

It is whether the workflow matches the way you want to make coffee.
