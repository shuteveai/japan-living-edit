An iron kettle and a cast-iron teapot can look similar at first glance.

Both may be heavy, dark, textured, and associated with Nambu ironware. For overseas buyers, that similarity can create a practical problem: not every cast-iron vessel used around tea is a tetsubin.

IWACHU is a useful example because the company distinguishes between iron kettles, teapots, and other Nambu tekki products on its official site.

The useful question is not which one is more traditional or more valuable.

**It is what each object is designed to do.**

## 1. A Tetsubin and a Cast-Iron Teapot Are Not the Same Thing

A traditional iron kettle, or tetsubin, is used to heat water.

A cast-iron teapot is generally used to brew and serve tea after hot water has already been prepared.

That difference changes everything: interior treatment, heat-source use, cleaning, drying, and ownership.

IWACHU's official product categories separate [iron kettles](https://iwachu.co.jp/en/pages/tetsubin) from [teapots](https://iwachu.co.jp/en/pages/kyusu). That separation is the first clue that the two should not be treated as interchangeable.

## 2. Start With the Interior

Interior treatment is one of the most important differences.

IWACHU explains that its teapots have an enameled interior. The purpose is practical: the teapot is made for brewing tea and serving it, not for boiling water over direct heat.

By contrast, IWACHU describes its iron kettles as having an oxide coating on the inside created through traditional kiln-firing.

That does not mean every Nambu cast-iron item follows one rule.

It means the interior must be understood by product type.

Before using any cast-iron vessel, check what the manufacturer says the inside is designed for.

## 3. Their Jobs Are Different

The job of a kettle is to heat water.

The job of a teapot is to receive hot water and tea leaves, then brew and pour tea.

Those jobs sound close, but they require different behavior from the object.

A kettle must deal with heat from below. A teapot must hold and pour brewed tea while protecting its interior treatment.

The confusion often comes from appearance.

A cast-iron teapot may look like a small kettle, but the intended use is not the same.

## 4. Can You Put Them on a Heat Source?

This is where product-specific instructions matter.

IWACHU's [teapot care page](https://iwachu.co.jp/en/pages/care-kyusu) states that its teapots cannot be used to boil water and should not be placed over an open flame because dry heating can cause enamel to peel.

For iron kettles, IWACHU's FAQ and care guidance discuss boiling water and initial use. Some iron kettles or ironware items may be compatible with induction, but IWACHU also warns that heat-source compatibility depends on the product and labeling.

The safe rule is simple:

**Do not infer heat-source compatibility from material alone.**

Read the specific product instructions.

## 5. How a Nambu Iron Kettle Is Made

IWACHU's official Japanese manufacturing page states that an iron kettle involves around 65 steps and that most are performed by hand.

The English manufacturing-process page describes steps such as design, mold making, pattern stamping, mold assembly, casting, mold removal, sand removal, coloring, and inspection.

Those process details help explain why a tetsubin is not just a generic cast-iron container.

But they still do not prove that every kettle is automatically better for every buyer.

Manufacturing process is evidence of how an object is made, not a guarantee that it matches every household's use.

## 6. Care Is Part of Ownership

Ironware asks for care.

IWACHU's iron-kettle guidance describes an initial process of rinsing, filling with water, boiling, and discarding the water several times until it becomes clear. After use, care instructions emphasize drying and avoiding moisture left on the object.

Teapot care is different because of the enamel interior.

IWACHU recommends washing the inside and tea strainer with cold or lukewarm water, wiping away moisture, and avoiding metal scrubbers or abrasives that may damage the enamel surface.

Care is not a side note.

It is part of choosing the object.

## 7. What About Rust?

Rust is one reason ironware can feel intimidating.

IWACHU's teapot care page notes that cast iron products may rust if moisture is left on them. Its FAQ also addresses rust in iron kettles and gives care guidance rather than treating rust as a simple product failure.

That does not mean rust should be ignored.

It means the owner should understand how the product is meant to be dried, stored, and maintained.

Traditional material does not remove the need for ordinary care.

## 8. Traditional Craft Does Not Mean Maintenance-Free

Nambu ironware carries craft and regional context, but that should not be turned into a fantasy of effortless ownership.

A tetsubin can require drying, break-in, and attention to water and heat.

An enameled teapot can require care to protect the interior surface.

Neither should be treated as a lifetime object simply because it is traditional.

The more useful lens is construction and intended use, the same approach used in our guide to [GEO PRODUCT cookware](/articles/inside-geo-product-japanese-cookware-seven-layers/).

## Which One Fits the Intended Use?

Choose a kettle if the goal is to heat water and you are prepared to follow kettle-specific care instructions.

Choose a cast-iron teapot if the goal is to brew and serve tea with hot water prepared elsewhere.

Do not choose based on appearance alone.

The interior and heat-source instructions are more important than the silhouette.

## Final Thoughts

A Nambu iron kettle and a cast-iron teapot are related objects, but they are not the same tool.

One is built around heating water.

The other is built around brewing tea.

That difference affects interior treatment, heat use, care, and maintenance.

The practical lesson is simple:

**Before buying a cast-iron tea object, ask what it is meant to do, not only what it looks like.**
